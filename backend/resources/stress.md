# Managing stress and burnout

## Separating what's urgent from what's important
When everything feels like it's on fire, it helps to sort tasks into two questions: does this need to happen today, and does this actually matter to me? Often only one or two things are truly both — the rest can wait or be let go of entirely.

## The value of a real break
A break spent scrolling a phone often doesn't feel restful, because the mind stays "on." A genuine break — stepping outside, stretching, closing your eyes for a few minutes without a screen — gives your nervous system an actual chance to reset, even if it's only five minutes.

## Noticing your stress signals early
Stress tends to build gradually: a tight jaw, shallow breathing, snapping at small things, trouble focusing. Catching these early signals gives you the chance to intervene before you hit full burnout, rather than waiting until you're already exhausted.

## Saying no as self-preservation
Taking on one more thing when you're already stretched thin doesn't make you more capable, it makes the whole load heavier. Protecting your time isn't selfish — it's often what allows you to keep showing up for the things that matter most.

## Micro-recovery through the day
You don't need a whole vacation to recover from stress. Short pockets of recovery — a two-minute stretch, stepping outside for fresh air, a few slow breaths between meetings — add up and can meaningfully lower the stress load you're carrying by the end of the day.
